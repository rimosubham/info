A founder profile · in his own arc

The traveler who became a founder.

Subham Paul is the founder, lead author, and chief route-finder at Offbeat Peak — the travel studio behind some of the more deliberate, less-photographed itineraries coming out of India today. His philosophy is straightforward: a good trip is not a checklist. It is a place that changes the person who visits it.

For nearly a decade before founding Offbeat Peak, Paul travelled — often quietly, often without a return ticket. Across India, Thailand, Vietnam, Indonesia, Malaysia, and Dubai, he developed a habit of arriving in famous cities only to leave them quickly, drawn instead to the villages, valleys, and back-alleys that the algorithm had not yet found. That instinct — toward the unmarked and the unmarketed — would, in time, become the entire business he built.

The route that became a company

"I was tired of selling my own time to companies whose travel I'd never want to do," Paul says. "And I was tired of being a customer of travel companies that hadn't actually been to the places they were selling." The contradiction nagged at him for years. In April 2023, after a packaged tour through Rajasthan he describes as "engineered for Instagram, not for memory," he returned to Delhi, called his co-founder Priya Kapoor, and within a week the spreadsheet that would become Offbeat Peak was open on three laptops.

The company's first listing was a homestay in Tirthan Valley belonging to a woman named Asha Ji, whose siddu Paul had eaten three years earlier on a trek and never forgotten. "The whole company is, in some sense, an attempt to repay her hospitality," he likes to say. The studio now works with 87 such homestay families across India and Southeast Asia — most of them found, vetted, or befriended by Paul himself.

"A good trip is not a checklist. It is a place that changes the person who visits it."

What he writes about

Beyond the company, Paul writes a journal column on Offbeat Peak's Stories section — a kind of slow, periodic dispatch from wherever he happens to be. The pieces are often short, occasionally long, and rarely on the obvious places. A typical recent entry: "Why I've stopped photographing temples", or "The Kuala Lumpur night market that doesn't appear on Google."

His writing has been informed by, and is sometimes cited by, peers in the Indian independent-travel space. He prefers reportage over recommendation, and the journal page has, perhaps unintentionally, become its own slow audience — picking up readers who don't even travel with Offbeat Peak but enjoy the dispatches anyway.

What he's working on

In April 2026, Paul is splitting time between the studio's Tirthan base and Havelock Island, where the team is finalising Offbeat Peak's Andaman launch (Q3 2026). A Nagaland chapter follows in December, timed to the Hornbill Festival. Beyond that, he is reportedly researching Bhutan and Sri Lanka for 2027, though both are still on the maybe-list.

Asked what success looks like, Paul tends to demur. "I don't want Offbeat Peak to be the biggest travel brand in India," he said in a recent interview. "I want it to be the one your trusted friend recommends. The one you come back to three times over five years. We'll be small. That's the whole point."
